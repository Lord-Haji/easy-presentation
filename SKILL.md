# Easy Presentation

Build talks that sound like a clear, grounded human speaking to a room, not an essay read aloud and not empty TEDx theater. Combine three pillars: **Steve Jobs' slide clarity**, **Will Stephen's spoken delivery mechanics**, and **strict academic rigor**.

---

## 1. Slide Design (Steve Jobs' Clarity)

- **One idea per slide.** Every slide answers one question. Split slides that try to make two unrelated points.
- **Cut clutter ruthlessly.** Keep content slides to **25–45 words** (excluding chart labels). Slides are visual anchors for the speaker, not a teleprompter or mini-paper.
- **One core message**, introduced in the first 30 seconds and echoed through the middle and ending.
- **Story-like flow:** Connect **Opening Hook -> Problem -> Method -> Key Findings -> Practical Impact**.
- **Simple visuals beat dense tables.** Use pictograms, clean bar charts, or 3 big-number cards on slides. Keep full regression tables, confidence intervals, and forest plots in the written report or backup slides.
- **No filler closing slides.** Skip "Thank you for your attention" slides and end directly on your takeaway slide.
- **Visual check every time.** Render slides to PNG and inspect every slide for title wrapping, overlapping boxes, or clipped text.

---

## 2. Human Spoken Delivery (Will Stephen's Craft, Minus the Hype)

Will Stephen's TEDxNewYork parody (*"How to Sound Smart in Your TEDx Talk"*) showed that **pacing, pauses, contrast, and physical cues** make an audience lean in, while mocking speakers who use them to disguise empty buzzwords. Keep the delivery craft and drop the hollow theater.

- **Write for the ear, not the eye.**
  - Keep spoken sentences short (8–16 words). Split nested clauses into two sentences.
  - Use natural contractions (`don't`, `it's`, `here's`, `that's`, `won't`) and plain verbs (`checked`, `caught`, `drops`, `shows`).
  - Do not read parenthetical statistics (`OR = 0.554, 95% CI [0.357, 0.862]`) out loud unless asked in Q&A. Say `"45 to 76 percent lower"` and drop your voice slightly at the end of key numbers so they land.
- **Strong 10-second opening.** Start with a concrete, relatable scene or puzzle that makes the audience care before reciting dataset counts.
- **Controlled pacing and purposeful pauses.**
  - Target **130–145 words per minute** (~400–430 words for a 3-minute talk).
  - Mark `[pause]` before and after your biggest number or contrast, `[slow down]` on the core message, and `[point]` when directing eyes to a visual on screen.
- **Vary sentence rhythm.** Avoid starting three slides in a row with `"First... Second... Third..."` or `"Next, we looked at..."`.
- **Ban empty TEDx buzzwords.** Cut `paradigm shift`, `synergy`, `disrupt`, `game changer`, `delve`, `leverage`, and fake-profound mic-drop endings.

---

## 3. Academic Standards You Must Maintain

Delivery without rigor breaks trust in a technical or academic room. Every presentation must keep:

1. **Rigor in data and interpretation:** Report fair within-group comparisons and effect sizes. Never hide Simpson's paradox, null findings, or negative results.
2. **Transparency about assumptions, limitations, and uncertainties:** State plainly who or what labeled the data (humans vs. AI agents), what a proxy measure cannot prove, and where a proposed method breaks down.
3. **Enough methodological detail to evaluate or replicate:** Include one clean, jargon-free visual slide showing how the data was filtered, audited, validated, and held out.
4. **Evidence-driven claims without hype:** Every claim on a slide must map to a verified number in the underlying results. Never oversell marginal or non-significant gains.
5. **Professional, calm tone:** Sound curious and honest, like a researcher sharing a real finding rather than a salesperson.

---

## 4. Workflow

1. **Write the 1-sentence core message** in plain English with zero textbook jargon.
2. **Map the story arc** (5–7 slides for a 3-minute talk; 10–12 for a 10-minute talk): Hook -> Problem -> Method -> Findings -> Practical Action -> Takeaway.
3. **Strip each slide down** until a viewer can scan it in under 4 seconds.
4. **Draft the spoken script** at 130–145 wpm with `[pause]`, `[point]`, and `[slow down]` cues. Read every line mentally as speech; rewrite anything you would not say to a classmate over coffee.
5. **Run `eval.md`** to verify slide clutter, spoken cadence, exact numbers, limitations, and zero hype.
6. **Prepare 3–4 short Q&A answers** for the toughest methodological questions a reviewer or professor will ask.
